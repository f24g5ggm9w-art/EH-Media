Clair Global Deploys Adamson VGt To Power D-Block Europe's "PTSD2" Arena Tour

The UK/Ireland run is powered by 74 VGt elements and 44 VGs subwoofers, filling rooms like the O2 and Co-op Live.

PSW Staff · August 2026

Clair Global has been tapped to provide an Adamson VGt line array system for D-Block Europe's "PTSD2" arena tour, as the UK hip-hop duo continues through Ireland and the UK in support of their recently released mixtape. The run included stops at 3Arena Dublin, OVO Hydro in Glasgow, M&S Bank Arena Liverpool, Co-op Live in Manchester, and multiple nights at London's O2.

The primary PA comprises 74 Adamson VGt elements and 44 VGs subwoofers, supported by eight CS7P and four CS10P subs, tied together on the network side by a Gateway Milan-compliant Ethernet switch. Monitor world runs on Adamson MG10 wedges, with CS118 and CS119 subwoofers on the stage side for low-end support.

The audio side of the tour is led by FOH engineer Raphael Williams and monitor engineer Morgan Moore, with production manager Femi Bello and systems engineer Aaron Boothe handling day-to-day system deployment across the run's arena-scale venues.

The VGt platform has become Clair's preferred solution for mid-size arena work that isn't quite large-format touring but still has to deliver output to venues like the O2 — without the truck count of a stadium-scale setup.

LD Systems Finishes 1,194-Box Fulcrum Acoustic Overhaul At NRG Stadium For The World Cup

Everything hangs from the stadium's fixed super truss, not the moving roof — a 24-year-old rigging solution the new system just inherited.

PSW Staff · August 2026

NRG Stadium in Houston was completely retooled by integrator LD Systems — which had already integrated the stadium's original sound system back in 2002 — working with consultants WJHW on behalf of the Houston Texans and Harris County, ahead of the seven games it will host during the 2026 World Cup. The new system includes 1,194 Fulcrum Acoustic loudspeakers throughout the bowl, concourses, and support spaces, including high-output horn-loaded AHC443 arrays and CS218LWR dual-18-inch weather-resistant subwoofers, plus CX1226-MT200, CX1226, CX1595, CX826-MT120, FH1566, and FH1595 models covering zones outside the main bowl.

The more interesting engineering story is where all of that hangs. NRG's roof is built from two independently retracting panels, so nothing can be rigged to the roof structure itself — it moves. Instead, the entire bowl system is anchored to the stadium's "super truss," a structural element separate from the roof that sits roughly 250 feet above the playing field. That constraint, not just weatherproofing, is what has shaped the audio system's design since 2002.

"Because this stadium has a retractable roof, we can't hang anything from it and therefore everything had to be mounted onto the super truss. This constraint defined both the original system design and upgrade." — Kevin Broussard, Sales Engineer, LD Systems

For the new loudspeakers, LD Systems reused the original custom rigging frames from the 2002 install to hoist them into the exact same positions on the super truss — frames engineered so boxes can be lowered for service without disturbing alignment. In 24 years, the team never actually had to lower one for maintenance. Every enclosure is also weather-rated, since the position stays exposed to the elements any time the roof is open. In the process — 250 feet up — LD Systems cut the system's overall weight by 40 percent compared to the previous rig.

The control side runs on a Q-SYS platform built around redundant Core 5200 processors for mission-critical reliability, with Core 610 processors handling suite-level audio separately. Amplification is split by zone: Powersoft Unica amplifiers drive the main bowl and concourse, while Crown DCI amplifiers cover clubs, back-of-house, and ancillary areas. Front of house runs through a Yamaha Rivage PM5, with wireless and IEM coordination handled by Shure Axient. That suite upgrade alone is its own story: more than 260 suites now get dedicated speakers, control panels, and selectable audio sources (bowl, TV, radio, postgame, Bluetooth), replacing bowl spill or TV feeds that used to lag by up to four seconds.

For a stadium of NRG's size, the full site-wide upgrade took roughly six months to complete — but the number that stands out is on the back end: final bowl-wide tuning was finished in a single six-hour session, ahead of a tournament that filled the stadium with up to 72,220 fans per match.

"Our deep knowledge of NRG Stadium has allowed us to be extremely precise during this exciting new phase of the building. We knew exactly what this job required and that familiarity ultimately saw us back on the project." — Kevin Broussard, Sales Engineer, LD Systems

Violet Audio Introduces dMix 128 Networked Digital Mixing System

128 channels, 88 buses, and sub-millisecond latency in a single rack-mount unit built for install and broadcast work.

PSW Staff · August 2026

Violet Audio has started shipping the dMix 128, a networked digital mixing system aimed at installations where reliability and channel count matter more than a physical control surface. The FPGA-based unit handles 128 channels and 88 buses at sample rates up to 96 kHz with sub-millisecond system latency, packed into a single rack-mount chassis with 32 mic/line inputs and 24 outputs.

Control runs through an HTML5 browser interface accessible from a computer or tablet, with no dedicated control surface required — a deliberate choice for fixed installs where a full physical console isn't practical or necessary. Networking includes MADI and AES67 I/O, and the unit ships with redundant power supplies and built-in effects processing.

The dMix 128 is available now at $5,995[1], positioning it as a networked alternative for install and broadcast applications that need console-grade routing and processing without console-scale hardware.

Three Different Rooms, Three Different Fixes — And One Diagnosis That Keeps Showing Up

A sanctuary in the shape of a diamond, an octagon, and a gym walked into 2026 with the same underlying problem: nobody had really designed the movement of sound within the room.

Marc Colbary · August 2026

I keep a loose file of worship audio install stories floating around, mostly because I like seeing how different integrators solve the same kind of problem with completely different gear. Three of these from this year stuck with me — not because they used the same platform (they didn't: EAW, d&b, and Martin Audio are all in play here) or even the same kind of fix, but because each one turned out to be a room-geometry problem hiding under a "we need a better system" diagnosis.

Three Rooms, Three Different Culprits

Earlier this year, PSW covered Christ Covenant Church in Tennessee, a diamond-shaped worship center with a vaulted roof that had spent years fighting uneven coverage and a harsh top end. The actual culprit here was low end: subwoofers sitting under the stage were creating room nodes and anti-nodes, dead spots and boomy spots that never lined up with where people actually sat. Four EAW AC6 ADAPTive columns went up in two vertical pairs, with the subs flown to a center-mono position between them. That solved the room's standing-wave problem, and the church now runs comfortably at 85-90 dB SPL with even coverage.

Central Assembly of God in Pennsylvania was beset with a completely different disease. Its 20-year-old system in an octagonal sanctuary wasn't fighting room modes — it was fighting geometry that a conventional left/right rig simply can't evenly cover. "With the old system, if it sounded good in the front, it was way too quiet in the back," integrator Brandon Cecil of Fresh Tech told PSW. The fix wasn't relocating anything low-frequency; it was a d&b Soundscape system built around a DS100 Matrix Processor running En-Scene spatial processing, driving five 24S point-source loudspeakers and eight 5S fills across the octagon, with six B6 subs run in inframode and an Allen & Heath Avantis at front of house. En-Scene gave each of the church's 14 musicians and 7 singers a distinct, fixed position in the mix — something the old system couldn't do structurally, no matter how loud it ran.

A third variant again is Fellowship Bible Church in Sewell, New Jersey, where a multipurpose gymnasium was pressed into service as a sanctuary, working against hard surfaces, exposed structure, and high ceilings. The fix wasn't directivity alone — two Martin Audio TORUS constant-curvature line arrays went in flown, paired with a single SX218 dual-18 subwoofer and four CDD15 loudspeakers covering the back of the room as delays, plus acoustic absorption panels on the room itself. It's a directivity-and-treatment story, not a boxes-only one.

Same Diagnosis, Different Prescription

None of these were "the system isn't loud enough" problems — that's the key, and it's especially true at Central Assembly and Fellowship. What was consistent across all three was the diagnostic step that came before any gear got specified: figuring out exactly how this room's shape was affecting the sound before choosing a fix. In Tennessee that meant tracing a harshness complaint back to where the low end actually sat. In Pennsylvania it meant recognizing the octagon's problem was about space, not loudness. In New Jersey it meant accepting that a line array alone wasn't going to beat a gym's hard, parallel walls, so acoustic treatment went in alongside the new system instead of getting left out of the budget.

This is a more helpful lesson for readers than "move your subs" would be: the strategy has to be tailored to the diagnosis, and the diagnosis is different every time the room is.

What I'd Watch For Next

I'd expect more coverage that leads with the diagnosis instead of the gear list — what went wrong in that room before naming the loudspeaker that fixed it. All three of these were planned system upgrades, not repairs, which suggests the diagnostic step is happening earlier in the process at churches that have the lead time to do it right rather than react to a failure.

Study Hall — Recording

AI Says It Can Mix Your Session. Here's Where I'd Actually Let It.

The tools are real. The decision still isn't theirs to make.

Marc Colbary · August 2026

With every DAW update this year, "AI-assisted" shows up somewhere, and I've seen engineers fall into two camps: the ones who assume it's all marketing, and the ones who've quietly started letting it handle more of the mundane parts of their session. Having spent time with a few of these tools, I think both camps are half right.

Where It Actually Earns Its Keep

Avid's Pro Tools 2026.4 update leaned further into AI-powered speech-to-text, which speeds up dialogue and lyric-driven workflows in a way that's genuinely hard to argue with — nobody's creative judgment is on the line when a machine transcribes a scratch vocal for editing reference. Likewise, the SoundFlow integration that landed in Pro Tools brought over 1,700 prebuilt macros with AI assistance for things like stem bouncing, track creation, and session navigation. That's the category where I think AI belongs right now: repetitive, mechanical, rules-based tasks that eat session time without requiring taste.

Session prep, file management, batch renaming, click-track generation, basic gain-riding on a dialogue pass for consistency before a human pass — all of it is fair game. It's the same reason nobody misses manually numbering audio regions anymore. Automating the tedious part of the job doesn't threaten the part that actually requires a trained ear.

Where I Still Want A Human

Where I get more skeptical is anything that touches a creative decision — EQ moves that are about the vibe of a mix rather than fixing a measurable problem, compression settings chosen for feel, level balances that are really about storytelling rather than loudness matching. Those decisions are downstream of a hundred small choices about what a song, or a sermon, or a broadcast is supposed to feel like to the person listening, and that's context a model doesn't have unless someone hands it to it explicitly, at which point you've mostly just built a very elaborate preset.

I'd also flag that "AI-assisted" is doing a lot of marketing work right now, and it's worth being specific in copy about what a feature actually automates versus what it merely suggests. Readers in this industry can tell the difference, and vague AI claims erode trust in the coverage faster than almost anything else I can think of.

The Practical Takeaway

My rule of thumb: if a task has a right answer that doesn't depend on taste, let the tool handle it. If the "right answer" is actually a matter of opinion, keep a human in the loop — and expect that to hold for a while yet, no matter how good the next model gets at pattern-matching what a good mix generally sounds like.
